Learning Machine Learning the Slow Way

How I stopped chasing hype, went back to basics, and finally started to understand the models I was building — without a PhD, but with a lot of patience.

The Slow Path I Chose

I first encountered machine learning the way many backend engineers do — through a tutorial titled "Build a Neural Network in 10 Minutes." I copied the code, ran it, and watched the accuracy numbers climb. I felt a rush of excitement. I thought I had unlocked a superpower.

That feeling lasted about a week. When I tried to apply the same approach to a real problem — predicting customer churn for a side project — my model performed abysmally. I didn't know why. I didn't know how to fix it. I realised that the 10-minute tutorial had given me a recipe, but it hadn't given me understanding.

I made a decision then: I would learn machine learning the slow way. No shortcuts. No copy-paste from Kaggle. I would build things from scratch, fail repeatedly, and force myself to understand the maths and the trade-offs. It took years. It was frustrating. But it was the best decision I've ever made as an engineer.

This is the story of that journey — the mistakes, the breakthroughs, and the quiet satisfaction of finally understanding what the models are actually doing.

My First Model: A Spectacular Failure

My first serious attempt was a logistic regression model to classify whether a customer would make a purchase. I had a dataset, I had scikit-learn, and I had confidence. I trained the model, evaluated it on my test set, and got an accuracy of 92%. I was ecstatic.

Then I deployed it. The real-world performance was barely above random guessing. I had made every classic mistake: I had trained on data with target leakage, I had ignored class imbalance, and I had evaluated on a test set that was too small and not representative of the real distribution.

That failure taught me the first hard lesson: your model is only as good as your data and your evaluation strategy. The algorithm itself is almost never the bottleneck.

I started over. I spent weeks cleaning the data, understanding each feature, and building a proper validation pipeline. I learned about stratified cross-validation, precision and recall, and the confusion matrix. The next model performed well in production, and I understood exactly why.

The Math I Had to Relearn (and Actually Enjoy)

I had taken calculus and linear algebra in university, but I had forgotten most of it. When I started reading blog posts about gradient descent and backpropagation, I realised I needed to go back to the basics.

I didn't enrol in a course. Instead, I implemented everything from scratch. I wrote a linear regression model using only NumPy, deriving the gradient descent update step by hand. I built a simple neural network with one hidden layer and manually computed the gradients. It was slow, and I made many mistakes, but each mistake forced me to understand the underlying maths.

Here's a snippet of the kind of code I wrote to truly understand backpropagation: 

import numpy as np

                                    class SimpleNN:
                                    def __init__(self, input_size, hidden_size,
                                    output_size):
                                    self.W1 = np.random.randn(input_size,
                                    hidden_size) * 0.01
                                    self.b1 = np.zeros((1, hidden_size))
                                    self.W2 = np.random.randn(hidden_size,
                                    output_size) * 0.01
                                    self.b2 = np.zeros((1, output_size))

                                    def sigmoid(self, x):
                                    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))

                                    def sigmoid_derivative(self, x):
                                    return x * (1 - x)

                                    def forward(self, X):
                                    self.z1 = np.dot(X, self.W1) + self.b1
                                    self.a1 = self.sigmoid(self.z1)
                                    self.z2 = np.dot(self.a1, self.W2) + self.b2
                                    self.a2 = self.sigmoid(self.z2)
                                    return self.a2

                                    def backward(self, X, y, learning_rate):
                                    m = X.shape[0]
                                    # Loss derivative
                                    dZ2 = self.a2 - y
                                    dW2 = (1/m) * np.dot(self.a1.T, dZ2)
                                    db2 = (1/m) * np.sum(dZ2, axis=0,
                                    keepdims=True)

                                    dA1 = np.dot(dZ2, self.W2.T)
                                    dZ1 = dA1 * self.sigmoid_derivative(self.a1)
                                    dW1 = (1/m) * np.dot(X.T, dZ1)
                                    db1 = (1/m) * np.sum(dZ1, axis=0,
                                    keepdims=True)

                                    # Update weights
                                    self.W2 -= learning_rate * dW2
                                    self.b2 -= learning_rate * db2
                                    self.W1 -= learning_rate * dW1
                                    self.b1 -= learning_rate * db1

Writing this code was tedious, but it demystified the entire process. Today, I use high-level frameworks like PyTorch, but I never treat them as black boxes. I know what the optimiser is doing, I understand the effect of different activation functions, and I can debug training curves with confidence.

The math isn't scary — it's just a language. And like any language, you learn it by speaking it, not by memorising vocabulary.

Data Quality Over Model Fancy

I used to believe that the best model would win. I'd spend weeks tuning hyperparameters, trying different architectures, and experimenting with exotic loss functions. Then a senior engineer on my team said something that changed my perspective: "A simple model with great data will always beat a complex model with mediocre data."

I started spending 80% of my time on data preparation. I wrote scripts to clean outliers, handle missing values, and normalise features. I built visualisations to understand distributions and correlations. I created synthetic examples for minority classes to address imbalance.

The results were dramatic. A simple logistic regression on well-prepared data often outperformed a random forest on raw data. Feature engineering became my favourite part of the process — creating new features from domain knowledge that captured patterns the raw data couldn't see.

One of my favourite tricks is the target encoding for high-cardinality categorical variables: 

def target_encode(df, column, target,
                                    min_samples=10):
                                    # Calculate mean target per category
                                    encoding = df.groupby(column)[target].mean()
                                    counts = df.groupby(column)[target].count()

                                    # Smooth with global mean to avoid overfitting
                                    global_mean = df[target].mean()
                                    smoothed = (encoding * counts + global_mean *
                                    min_samples) / (counts + min_samples)

                                    df[column + '_encoded'] =
                                    df[column].map(smoothed)
                                    return df

This simple encoding, with smoothing to prevent overfitting, consistently improved my model performance without adding any complexity to the algorithm. Data quality is the great equaliser in machine learning.

Overfitting and the Validation Wake-Up Call

I spent a week building a deep learning model that achieved 99% accuracy on my training set. I was thrilled. Then I ran it on the validation set and got 67%. I had committed the cardinal sin of machine learning: I had tuned my model to the training data until it memorised the noise.

Overfitting is the Siren's song of ML. It seduces you with impressive numbers on the data you have, while failing on the data you don't. I learned to split my data properly — training, validation, and holdout test sets — and never to look at the test set until the very end.

I also discovered the power of early stopping and regularisation. Instead of training for a fixed number of epochs, I monitor the validation loss and stop when it starts to increase. In PyTorch: 

best_val_loss = float('inf')
                                    patience = 5
                                    patience_counter = 0

                                    for epoch in range(max_epochs):
                                    train_loss = train_one_epoch(model, train_loader)
                                    val_loss = evaluate(model, val_loader)

                                    if val_loss < best_val_loss:
                                    best_val_loss = val_loss
                                    patience_counter = 0
                                    torch.save(model.state_dict(), 'best_model.pt')
                                    else:
                                    patience_counter += 1
                                    if patience_counter >= patience:
                                    print("Early stopping triggered")
                                    break

Another lesson: simpler models generalise better. I used to think that more layers, more neurons, and more complex architectures were always better. Now, I start with the simplest model that could possibly work and only add complexity when the data demands it. Occam's razor applies to ML more than any other field.

From Notebook to Maintainable Code

Jupyter notebooks are wonderful for exploration. But they're terrible for production. I learned this the hard way when I tried to deploy a model that was buried in a massive notebook with cells that had to be run in a specific order.

I rewrote the entire pipeline as a modular Python package. I separated data loading, feature engineering, model training, and evaluation into distinct modules. I wrote unit tests for each component. I added logging and configuration management. The process took three times longer than the original notebook, but it saved me countless hours of debugging later.

Here's the structure I use now: 

ml_project/
                                    ├── data/
                                    │   ├── loader.py
                                    │   └── preprocess.py
                                    ├── features/
                                    │   ├── build.py
                                    │   └── transforms.py
                                    ├── models/
                                    │   ├── train.py
                                    │   └── predict.py
                                    ├── evaluation/
                                    │   └── metrics.py
                                    ├── pipelines/
                                    │   └── full_pipeline.py
                                    └── config.yaml

I also adopted MLflow for experiment tracking. I log every model run with its hyperparameters, metrics, and a hash of the training data. This lets me reproduce any experiment months later and compare results objectively. It's the engineering discipline that makes ML sustainable.

The shift from "notebook scientist" to "ML engineer" was uncomfortable, but it made my work more reliable and collaborative. Code that is well-structured is code that can be trusted.

The Deployment Reality Check

Building a model is one thing. Deploying it is a completely different beast. My first deployment was a disaster. The model worked perfectly in my development environment, but in production, it crashed with obscure dependency errors, consumed too much memory, and was too slow for real-time requests.

I learned about the importance of model serialisation (using joblib or ONNX), containerisation with Docker, and serving infrastructure like TorchServe or FastAPI. Here's the FastAPI pattern I use now: 

from fastapi import FastAPI, HTTPException
                                    import numpy as np
                                    import joblib

                                    app = FastAPI()
                                    model = joblib.load('model.joblib')
                                    scaler = joblib.load('scaler.joblib')

                                    @app.post("/predict")
                                    async def predict(data: dict):
                                    try:
                                    features = np.array(data['features']).reshape(1,
                                    -1)
                                    scaled = scaler.transform(features)
                                    prediction = model.predict(scaled)
                                    return {"prediction": int(prediction[0])}
                                    except Exception as e:
                                    raise HTTPException(status_code=400,
                                    detail=str(e))

                                    @app.get("/health")
                                    async def health():
                                    return {"status": "ok"}

I also added monitoring — tracking prediction drift, input distributions, and model latency. A model in production isn't a static artefact; it's a living system that needs to be watched and maintained. The deployment phase taught me more about system design than any book ever did.

One more lesson: always have a fallback. If your model service goes down, or returns garbage, your system should degrade gracefully. I implemented a simple rule-based fallback that kicks in when the model's confidence is too low or the service times out. It's not perfect, but it's better than failing entirely.

Staying Curious in a Hype-Driven World

The hardest part of learning ML today is filtering the noise. Every week, a new model is announced with state-of-the-art results. Every month, a new framework promises to revolutionise the field. It's easy to feel overwhelmed and inadequate.

I made peace with this by focusing on fundamentals. I don't need to know every new architecture. I need to understand the core principles — how models learn, how to evaluate them, how to turn a business problem into a machine learning problem. These fundamentals never go out of style.

I also found a community of engineers who share my approach — people who value understanding over hype. We share papers, discuss implementations, and review each other's code. This has been invaluable. The best way to learn is to teach, and I've learned more from explaining concepts to others than from any lecture.

I still read papers, but I read them critically. I ask myself: "What problem does this solve? What are the assumptions? What would it take to replicate this?" Most papers don't survive this scrutiny, and that's fine. The ones that do become part of my mental toolkit.

"In the rush to build the next big thing, don't forget to understand the thing you just built."

My curiosity extends beyond algorithms. I'm fascinated by the ethics of ML — how bias creeps into data, how models can amplify inequality, how to build systems that are fair and transparent. These are the questions that will define the field in the coming decade, and they deserve as much attention as any technical detail.

Final Thoughts

Learning machine learning the slow way has been one of the most rewarding challenges of my career. I'm not the fastest coder or the most mathematically gifted engineer. But I am persistent, and I am curious, and those two qualities have carried me further than any shortcut ever could.

If you're starting your journey, my advice is this: ignore the hype. Build a linear regression from scratch. Understand the bias- variance trade-off. Spend time on data quality. Write tests for your pipeline. And never, ever stop asking "why?".

The slow path is not the easy path. But it is the path that leads to real understanding. And real understanding is what separates someone who uses ML from someone who truly knows it.

I'm still on that path. Every project teaches me something new. Every failure reveals a gap in my knowledge. And every success reminds me why I fell in love with this field in the first place. It's not about the algorithms. It's about the problems we solve and the people we help. That's what keeps me going.
