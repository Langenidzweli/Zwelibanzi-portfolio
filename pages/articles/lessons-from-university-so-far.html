Lessons from University, So Far

A personal reflection on the modules that shaped me, the marks that humbled me, and the quiet realisation that the real education happened outside the lecture hall.

The Transcript That Told a Story

I recently opened my academic transcript for the first time in a while. It was a strange experience — seeing all those numbers laid out in neat columns, each one representing a semester of late nights, caffeine overdoses, and the quiet thrill of finally understanding something that had seemed impossible weeks before.

My first year was a wake-up call. I had arrived at university with the cocky assumption that I was smart enough to coast. Then Calculus handed me a 58%, and Probability Theory gave me the same. I remember staring at those marks and feeling a knot in my stomach. I had never failed at anything academic before. I didn't even know how to process it.

But that's the thing about a transcript — it's not a verdict. It's a map of a journey. You can see where I stumbled (Calculus, Probability, Distribution Theory), and you can see where I found my footing (Object Oriented Programming at 88%, Algebra at 87%, Operating Systems at 86%). The numbers don't tell you about the late-night debugging sessions, the study groups that became friendships, or the moment I realised I actually loved building software.

This article is about what I've learned from that journey — not just the content of the modules, but the lessons about learning, persistence, and the kind of engineer I want to become.

First Year: The Humbling

First year was brutal in the best possible way. I took Mechanics (71%) and Introduction to Programming (66%), and I thought I was doing fine. Then Calculus (58%) and Probability Theory (58%) hit me like a truck. I had never scored that low in my life. I spent weeks questioning whether I belonged in this degree at all.

Looking back, I can see that those low marks weren't a reflection of my ability. They were a reflection of my approach. I was trying to memorise formulas instead of understanding concepts. I was studying to pass exams, not to learn. It took me the entire first year to unlearn that habit.

But there were bright spots. Introduction to Numerical Methods & Mathematical Modelling gave me an 80%, and I remember the feeling of completing that project — it was the first time I had built something that actually did something useful. Object Oriented Programming (88%) was where I started to feel at home. The idea of modelling the world through objects and classes just clicked. I spent weekends building small projects, not because I had to, but because I enjoyed it.

Algebra (87%) was another surprise. I had always thought of algebra as abstract and disconnected from reality. But once I started seeing it as a language for describing structure, it became beautiful. That 87% felt earned — I had worked for it, and I understood it.

The lesson from first year was simple: you have to learn how to learn. The content is secondary. The skill of breaking down a complex topic, finding the right resources, and persevering through confusion — that's what you're really there to learn.

Second Year: Finding My Rhythm

Second year was when I started to figure out what I was actually good at. Operating Systems (86%) was a revelation. Suddenly, the magic of computers started to make sense. I understood processes, memory management, and file systems in a way that felt concrete and practical. It wasn't just theory anymore — it was the foundation of every system I would ever build.

Systems Analysis and Design (90%) was my highest mark, and I think it was because it combined two things I loved: problem-solving and communication. It wasn't just about writing code; it was about understanding what the user actually needed and designing a system that delivered it. I remember spending hours on the requirements gathering, mapping out data flows, and sketching architecture diagrams. It felt like I was being trained to be an engineer, not just a programmer.

Algorithm Analysis and Program Design (83%) was where theory met practice. I had always enjoyed writing code, but this module taught me to think about efficiency, trade-offs, and the beauty of a well-designed algorithm. I started reading about data structures in my free time. I began to appreciate the elegance of a red-black tree and the simplicity of a hash map.

Advanced Calculus (76%) and Statistical Inference (77%) were solid improvements from my first-year maths performances. I had learned to study smarter, to seek help when I was stuck, and to practice consistently. The marks weren't outstanding, but they were honest — they reflected the effort I had put in and the growth I had made.

The lessons from second year were about finding your strengths and leaning into them. I wasn't going to be a mathematician, and that was okay. I was going to be an engineer who understood enough maths to build robust systems. Once I stopped comparing myself to the pure maths students and started focusing on my own path, everything got easier.

Third Year: The Specialisations

Third year was when things got really interesting. Networks, Data Communication and Security (76%) gave me a glimpse into the world of distributed systems. I learned about TCP/IP, routing protocols, and the basics of network security. It was a reminder that software doesn't exist in a vacuum — it runs on networks, and those networks have their own constraints and vulnerabilities.

Software Engineering (82%) was the module I had been waiting for. It was everything I loved about Systems Analysis and Design, but deeper. Agile methodologies, version control, testing strategies, and project management — it was like learning the language of the professional world. I started working on group projects with a different mindset. I wasn't just writing code anymore; I was building a product with a team.

Regression Analysis (70%) and Non-Parametric Statistics (76%) were my bridge into data science. I started to see how statistical methods could be applied to real-world problems. It wasn't always intuitive, but I began to appreciate the rigor of hypothesis testing and the value of uncertainty quantification.

And then there were the modules I started but hadn't finished yet — Special Topics in Computer Science, Artificial Intelligence, and Markov. These were the frontier for me. AI was where I was headed next, and I could feel the excitement building.

The lesson from third year, so far, is that specialisation is a choice, not a destiny. You don't have to pick one thing and stick with it forever. You can explore, pivot, and combine disciplines in ways that feel authentic to you. I was building a foundation in software engineering while simultaneously discovering a love for AI and data. That felt like exactly where I was supposed to be.

What the Marks Didn't Tell Me

The transcript is a story of numbers, but it omits the most important parts. It doesn't show the hours I spent in the library with a study group, breaking down algorithms together. It doesn't show the first time I deployed a web application and felt the thrill of seeing it live. It doesn't show the friendships I built with people who shared my curiosity and frustration.

The marks don't tell you about the imposter syndrome I felt after scoring 58% in Calculus. They don't show the quiet confidence that built up over time as I realised that failure wasn't a reflection of who I was — it was a step on the path to mastery. They don't show the joy of finally understanding something that had confused you for months.

I learned more from the struggles than from the successes. The 58% in Probability Theory taught me humility. It taught me that some concepts require time and repetition. It taught me to ask for help without shame. The 90% in Systems Analysis was rewarding, but the 70% in Regression Analysis was more valuable because I had to work for it.

My transcript shows a clear upward trend in my computer science modules. Operating Systems (86%), Algorithm Analysis (83%), Software Engineering (82%) — these were the modules where I felt truly engaged. They were also the modules where I spent the most time outside of class, building side projects and reading beyond the syllabus. I realised that the best learning happens when you're doing something you actually care about.

I also learned that I'm not a pure mathematician. And that's okay. I'm an engineer who uses maths to build things. That distinction is subtle, but it's everything. It's the difference between deriving a proof and implementing a solution. Both are valid, but they require different mindsets and different skills.

The Modules That Shaped My Path

If I look back at the modules that had the most impact on who I am today, a few stand out:

  • Object Oriented Programming (88%): This was where I fell in love with software. The idea of modelling the world through objects and classes just made sense to me. I started building small projects for fun, and I never really stopped.
  • Operating Systems (86%): This module demystified the computer for me. I understood what was happening under the hood. It made me a better debugger and a more confident engineer.
  • Systems Analysis and Design (90%): This taught me that software engineering is about people as much as it is about code. The best systems are built on a deep understanding of the problem and the users.
  • Algorithm Analysis and Program Design (83%): I learned to think about complexity and trade-offs. I started to appreciate the elegance of a well-designed algorithm.
  • Software Engineering (82%): This was my first real taste of professional practice. Agile, testing, version control — it all started to come together.

But the module I'm most excited about is the one I'm still in the middle of — Artificial Intelligence. It's the frontier. It's where I feel like I'm learning something that truly pushes the boundaries of what's possible. I'm not sure where it will lead, but I'm excited to find out.

The Soft Skills That Aren't on the Transcript

University taught me a lot of things that aren't reflected in my marks. I learned how to collaborate with people who have different strengths and weaknesses. I learned how to manage my time, prioritise tasks, and deal with ambiguity. I learned that asking for help is a sign of strength, not weakness.

I also learned how to handle failure. Calculus (58%) was a failure by any standard. But it didn't define me. I took the feedback, adjusted my approach, and improved. The next year, Advanced Calculus (76%) was a significant step up. The progress wasn't linear, but it was real.

I learned the value of a study group. Some of my best learning happened not in the lecture hall but in the library, working through problems with friends. We pushed each other, explained concepts to each other, and celebrated each other's successes. Those relationships were as valuable as any module.

I learned to be comfortable with not knowing everything. The field is vast, and I'm only at the beginning. But I've also learned that curiosity is a skill you can cultivate. The more you learn, the more you realise how much you don't know — and that's a good thing. It means you're growing.

The transcript shows the grades. It doesn't show the growth. But I know it's there, because I can see it in the way I approach problems now compared to three years ago. I think more carefully. I ask better questions. I persist longer.

What I Would Tell My First-Year Self

If I could go back and talk to the version of me who opened that first transcript and saw 58% in Calculus, I would say this:

It's going to be okay. Those numbers don't define you. They're just data points. They tell you what you need to work on, not who you are. You're going to find subjects that click — OOP, Algebra, Operating Systems. You're going to find your people. You're going to build things that make you proud.

Stop comparing yourself to others. Some people are going to breeze through Calculus. Some are going to struggle with OOP. Everyone has their own path. Focus on yours. Learn at your own pace. Celebrate your own progress.

Ask for help. I spent too long trying to figure things out on my own. There are professors, teaching assistants, and classmates who are eager to help. Use them. It's not cheating. It's learning.

Build things. The lectures and assignments are important, but the real learning happens when you apply what you've learned to a project that matters to you. Start a side project. Contribute to open-source. Build a portfolio of things you're proud of.

Enjoy the journey. University is a unique time. You have the space to explore, make mistakes, and discover what you're passionate about. Don't rush through it. Savour the late-night study sessions. Appreciate the friends you're making. This is the foundation of everything that comes next.

If I could tell my first-year self one more thing, it would be this: your 58% in Calculus is not your ceiling; it's just your starting point. Keep going.

Final Thoughts

University, so far, has been a journey of discovering who I am as a learner and as an engineer. It's been about the modules I aced (Systems Analysis at 90%, OOP at 88%) and the ones that challenged me (Calculus at 58%, Probability at 58%). It's been about the upward trend in my CS marks and the realisation that I'm on the right path.

The transcript is a document, but the learning is something else entirely. It's the quiet confidence that comes from struggling through a difficult problem and finally solving it. It's the friendships forged in late-night study sessions. It's the excitement of discovering a field you love — for me, that was software engineering, and more recently, artificial intelligence.

I don't know what the rest of my university journey will hold. I have Special Topics, AI, and Markov still ahead of me. I'm curious to see what I'll learn, who I'll meet, and what I'll build. I'm excited for the challenges and the discoveries. And I'm grateful for the lessons I've already learned.

If you're reading this and you're in the middle of your own journey — whether you're acing everything or struggling with a particular module — my advice is simple: keep going. The struggle is part of the process. The marks are just milestones. The real reward is the person you become along the way.

I'm still becoming that person. And I wouldn't have it any other way.
