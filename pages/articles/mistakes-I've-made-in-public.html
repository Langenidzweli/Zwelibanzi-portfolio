Mistakes I've Made, in Public

A brutally honest recount of the bugs I shipped, the bad decisions I made, and the embarrassing moments that taught me more about engineering than any success ever could.

Why I'm Writing This

I've made a lot of mistakes. Some small. Some spectacular. Most of them in full view of colleagues, managers, and sometimes the entire internet. I've shipped code that broke in production, designed systems that collapsed under load, and given presentations that I still cringe about years later.

For a long time, I tried to hide these mistakes. I'd quietly fix the bug and hope no one noticed. I'd reframe a bad decision as a "learning experience" without admitting it was actually a failure. I'd avoid talking about the embarrassing moments in case they diminished people's perception of my competence.

But I've come to realise that the mistakes are the most valuable part of my journey. They're the moments that forced me to grow. They're the stories that make me a better mentor and a more empathetic engineer. And they're the things that, when shared, help others avoid the same pitfalls.

This article is my public confession. These are the mistakes I've made, in the open, with the hope that you might learn from them — or at least feel less alone in your own failures.

The Optimistic Deadline That Killed My Weekend

Early in my career, I was asked to estimate how long a feature would take. I was confident. I had built something similar before. I said, "Three days." The project manager wrote it down, and I felt a swell of pride. I was the developer who could deliver fast.

Three days came and went. The feature wasn't finished. I worked through the weekend, cutting corners to make it work. I deployed on Monday morning, exhausted and relieved. Then the bug reports started coming in. Something I had rushed caused a cascade of failures in unrelated parts of the system.

I had made the classic mistake: optimism bias. I had estimated the best-case scenario and ignored the inevitable obstacles. I had let my ego drive my estimate instead of my experience. And I had paid the price with a ruined weekend and a damaged reputation.

The lesson was painful but clear: always add a buffer. Estimate what you think it will take, then multiply by 1.5 or 2. Account for the unknown unknowns. And never, ever let the desire to look good override the need to be realistic. The trust you lose from a missed deadline is much harder to regain than the respect you might gain from a conservative estimate.

I now use a simple framework for estimating: 

// My estimation formula today
                                    // 1. Break down the work into small tasks
                                    // 2. Estimate each task in hours
                                    // 3. Add 50% for unexpected issues
                                    // 4. Add 20% for context switching and
                                    meetings
                                    // 5. Round up to the nearest day

                                    function estimate(taskList) {
                                    let totalHours = 0;
                                    for (let task of taskList) {
                                    totalHours += task.estimate;
                                    }
                                    let withBuffer = totalHours * 1.5;
                                    let withOverhead = withBuffer * 1.2;
                                    return Math.ceil(withOverhead / 8); // days
                                    }

It's not perfect, but it's much closer to reality than my naive early estimates. And it's saved me from many more ruined weekends.

The Production Outage I Caused

This one still stings. I was a junior developer, and I had been given a seemingly simple task: add a new field to a database table. I wrote the migration script, tested it in my development environment, and deployed it to production. Within minutes, the entire application started throwing exceptions. The site was down for forty-five minutes.

The problem was that I had added a NOT NULL constraint to a column that had existing NULL values in production. My development database had been populated with test data that didn't have the issue, so I never saw the failure. I had committed the cardinal sin of database migrations: never assume your test data matches production.

I sat at my desk, face burning, as the team scrambled to roll back the migration. The senior engineer who helped me fix it was calm and professional, but I could see the disappointment in his eyes. I had broken the service in public, and there was no hiding it.

That day changed how I approach migrations forever. Now, every migration script I write has:

  • A dry-run mode that shows what will change without executing it.
  • A rollback plan written before the migration is executed.
  • A validation step that checks for existing data that would violate the new constraints.

Here's the pattern I use for safe migrations in SQL: 

-- Step 1: Add the column as nullable first
                                    ALTER TABLE users ADD COLUMN timezone VARCHAR(50);

                                    -- Step 2: Backfill data for existing rows
                                    UPDATE users SET timezone = 'UTC' WHERE timezone IS
                                    NULL;

                                    -- Step 3: Verify no nulls remain
                                    SELECT COUNT(*) FROM users WHERE timezone IS NULL;
                                    -- This should return 0

                                    -- Step 4: Add the NOT NULL constraint
                                    ALTER TABLE users ALTER COLUMN timezone SET NOT NULL;

The forty-five minutes of downtime was humiliating. But it made me a better engineer. I now treat every migration with the respect it deserves, and I've never caused another outage from a schema change.

The Architecture I Over-Engineered

I was once tasked with building a simple notification service. The requirements were straightforward: send an email when certain events occurred. I could have built it with a single Spring Boot service and a transactional outbox pattern. Instead, I built a microservices architecture with three separate services, a message queue, and a separate notification service.

I was so excited about the architecture that I didn't stop to ask the most important question: what problem am I actually solving? The simple notification service didn't need the complexity. It needed to work reliably and be easy to maintain. Instead, I created a system that was difficult to debug, slow to develop, and expensive to run.

The worst part was that I presented this architecture to my team with enthusiasm. I drew diagrams. I explained the benefits of loose coupling and eventual consistency. I was convincing — and I was wrong. The team went along with it because I was the "expert" on the project.

A few months later, we were still struggling to ship the first feature. The complexity had slowed us down so much that we were barely making progress. The project was eventually rewritten as a single, simple service. I had wasted months of effort and eroded my team's trust.

The lesson was brutal but essential: start with the simplest thing that could possibly work. You can always add complexity later, when you actually need it. Adding complexity too early is like building a skyscraper when a bungalow would do. It's not clever; it's wasteful.

I now ask myself three questions before any architectural decision:

  1. Is this complexity necessary today?
  2. What is the cost of not having this complexity?
  3. Could we defer this decision until we have more data?

Most of the time, the answer to all three is "no". And that's when I know I'm on the right track.

The Code Review I Botched

I once gave a code review that was devastatingly bad. A junior developer had submitted a pull request with a reasonable implementation. But I was in a bad mood, and I picked apart every line. I called out minor style issues as if they were critical bugs. I suggested a completely different approach that would have required a full rewrite. I wrote comments that were dismissive and, frankly, unkind.

The developer didn't push back. They made the changes I requested, but I could see the enthusiasm drain from their face. The PR sat in review for a week. When it finally merged, the code was worse than the original implementation — and it was my fault.

The senior engineer on the team pulled me aside later and said, "That review wasn't helpful. It was about you, not about the code." She was right. I had let my ego and my frustration get in the way of mentoring someone. I had created a hostile environment instead of a learning one.

That conversation changed how I approach code reviews. Now, every review I write follows a simple rule: assume good faith and be kind. I focus on the code, not the person. I ask questions instead of making demands. I provide context for my suggestions. And I always find something to praise.

Here's the template I use in my code review comments now: 

// Code Review Comment Template
                                    // 1. Acknowledge something positive
                                    // "I really like how you structured this
                                    method."

                                    // 2. Ask clarifying questions
                                    // "I'm curious about the choice to use X
                                    instead of Y — what was the reasoning?"

                                    // 3. Suggest improvements with rationale
                                    // "Would you consider refactoring this loop
                                    into a stream? It might be more readable and
                                    easier to test."

                                    // 4. Offer to help
                                    // "If you'd like to pair on this, I'm
                                    available after stand-up."

I still make mistakes in code reviews. But I no longer make the mistake of being cruel. The junior developer I failed is now a senior engineer, and we have a good relationship. But I think about that review often — it reminds me that how you give feedback matters as much as the feedback itself.

The Presentation I Bombed

I was asked to give a technical talk at a company-wide engineering meeting. I was excited — this was my chance to show what I knew. I prepared slides, practiced my delivery, and walked into the room with confidence.

Then the questions started. A senior architect asked something I hadn't considered. I fumbled. Someone else pointed out a flaw in my reasoning. I tried to recover, but I dug myself deeper. My face burned. My voice wavered. By the end of the talk, I had lost the room entirely.

I spent the rest of the day replaying the presentation in my head. I was convinced everyone thought I was incompetent. I avoided eye contact with the people who had asked questions. I seriously considered quitting.

The lesson I eventually learned was that presentations are not about being right; they're about being humble and curious. When someone asks a question you can't answer, you say, "That's a great question — I don't know the answer, but I'll find out." When someone points out a flaw, you say, "Thank you for catching that — let me think about it."

I also learned to prepare for the tough questions. Before every presentation now, I write down the worst possible questions someone could ask, and I prepare answers. I also acknowledge the limitations of my arguments upfront, so I control the framing instead of being ambushed by it.

The bombed presentation was humiliating. But it taught me that vulnerability is not weakness. The next time I gave a talk, I started with a slide that listed the things I was still figuring out. The room relaxed. People engaged more. I had turned my biggest weakness into a strength.

The Performance Issue I Missed

I once shipped a feature that worked perfectly in development. It passed all the tests. It had been reviewed by my team. It was deployed, and I moved on to the next thing. A month later, the application started experiencing mysterious timeouts. Users were complaining. The performance team was pulling their hair out.

The root cause was a database query I had written. It worked fine with a few hundred records, but in production, with millions of records, it was a disaster. I had forgotten to add an index on a foreign key column. The query was doing a full table scan for every request. The database was struggling to keep up, and the application was failing under load.

The embarrassing part was that I had reviewed the query with a senior engineer, and neither of us had noticed. We were both guilty of the same oversight: we had assumed the query would perform well based on local testing, and we had ignored the reality of production scale.

The fix was trivial — a single index statement. But the damage was done. I had wasted hours of the performance team's time and caused days of degraded service for our users. I added an index and deployed it in minutes. The problem disappeared instantly.

The lesson was clear: test with production-like data volumes. If you can't replicate production scale, at least think about how your code will behave when it's under real load. And never, ever assume that a query that works on a small dataset will work on a large one.

I now have a checklist for every database query I write: 

// Database Query Checklist
                                    // 1. Is there an index on the columns used
                                    in the WHERE clause?
                                    // 2. Is there an index on the columns used
                                    in ORDER BY?
                                    // 3. Are there any joins? Do they have
                                    indexes on the join columns?
                                    // 4. What is the EXPLAIN plan? Does it show
                                    an index scan or a table scan?
                                    // 5. How does this query behave with 10x the
                                    current data volume?
                                    // 6. Is there a way to paginate the results
                                    to limit the data loaded?

That checklist has saved me from many more performance issues. But I still remember the feeling of watching the database struggle because of my oversight. It was a lesson I won't forget.

The Time I Was Wrong About the Framework

I once spent a week arguing against using a particular framework on a project. I had used an earlier version of it years ago, and it had been a nightmare. I was convinced this new version would be the same. I wrote long emails. I drew diagrams of how the framework would fail. I was loud and persistent.

The team, including the senior architect, disagreed with me. They had evaluated the framework and believed it was a good fit for the project. I was so certain I was right that I couldn't see the possibility that I might be wrong.

The project went ahead with the framework, over my objections. A year later, it was one of the most successful projects the company had ever built. The framework had improved dramatically. The team had been right, and I had been wrong — spectacularly wrong.

I had to go to the team and admit my mistake. It was one of the hardest conversations I've ever had. But it taught me a lesson that has shaped my career: being wrong is not a weakness; refusing to learn from it is.

I now approach technical debates with a different mindset. Instead of trying to be right, I try to be curious about being wrong. I ask questions instead of making pronouncements. I assume that other people have information I don't have. And I'm always open to changing my mind when the evidence shows I'm wrong.

The framework that I was wrong about is now a tool I use regularly. And every time I use it, I'm reminded of the importance of humility in engineering.

The Thing I Said on Twitter

I don't want to go into specifics, but I've posted things on social media that I regretted. I've been too opinionated. I've been dismissive of other people's work. I've written things that, in retrospect, made me look arrogant and unkind.

The worst part is that the internet never forgets. A comment you made five years ago can resurface and embarrass you again. I've learned that public writing is permanent writing, and I've become much more careful about what I put out there.

The lesson isn't to stop writing publicly. It's to write with empathy and humility. Before I post anything now, I ask myself: "Would I be comfortable saying this to someone's face? Would I be comfortable if they read it ten years from now?" If the answer is no, I don't post it.

I've also learned to apologise publicly when I'm wrong. A sincere, specific apology can repair a lot of damage. People remember how you handled a mistake more than the mistake itself.

The Mistake I Still Make

Here's the truth: I still make mistakes. Every day. I still ship bugs. I still make bad estimates. I still say the wrong thing in a meeting. I still get defensive when I shouldn't. The difference is that I've stopped pretending I don't.

I've learned to fail fast and fail openly. When I make a mistake, I acknowledge it immediately. I apologise if I've affected someone else. I fix the problem. And I document what I learned. This approach has earned me more trust than being perfect ever could.

I've also learned that mistakes are data. Every bug I've shipped has taught me something about my testing process. Every bad estimate has taught me something about my planning. Every embarrassing moment has taught me something about how I want to show up in the world.

Final Thoughts

I've made a lot of mistakes, and I've made most of them in public. It's been uncomfortable, humiliating, and sometimes painful. But it's also been the most effective way to learn.

The engineers I admire most are not the ones who never make mistakes. They're the ones who make mistakes gracefully. They acknowledge them. They learn from them. They share their lessons with others. They're human, and they're not afraid to show it.

If you're reading this and you're embarrassed about a mistake you made, I want you to know that it's okay. You're not alone. The best engineers I know have made much bigger mistakes than you have. They've shipped bugs that cost millions. They've designed architectures that failed catastrophically. They've said things they regret. And they're still here, still learning, still growing.

The only real mistake is the one you don't learn from. So learn from yours. Share them with others. And keep going. The best engineers aren't the ones who never fall — they're the ones who keep getting back up.

I'm still getting back up. And I'll probably keep making mistakes in public for a long time. I hope you'll join me.
